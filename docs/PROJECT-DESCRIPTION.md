# EIN. MEER. VON. SAND.


A monument records the stopping of sand. The program puts its letters back into motion.

EIN. MEER. VON. SAND. takes the surviving Danish and German inscriptions of the Sand Drift Monument at Tisvilde, together with English translations, and reads them through a common numerical rule. The stone commemorates the containment of drifting sand in 1738. On the screen, its account of stabilisation becomes a process of dispersal: words sound, loosen, and settle into a sea of letters.

The three columns share a screen without becoming equivalent. German appears on the left, Danish in the centre, English on the right. The English voice carries translations of both surviving languages. Their entrances overlap; related words activate a shared visual motif. Sand, wind, the sea, royal names, and the date 1738 return across differences of spelling and syntax.

Alphabet / partial

Each letter selects a harmonic of A2, 110 Hz. Its position in the alphabet is reduced modulo a number that moves, in steps, from twelve to seventeen. A zero remainder selects the modulus itself. Æ, Ø and Å extend the alphabet to twenty-nine positions. Vowels swell and hold; consonants strike. Recurrence within a line gives a letter its weight.

The changing modulus means that a returning letter need not return to the same frequency. Its earlier sounding remains in the drone. The present is heard against a residue of previous readings, while the visual field fills with characters that have left the text. Sediment is the work's memory, and the sea grows from what the reading has already used.

A third face

The monument has a Latin face as well. In my account of the site it has become unreadable under moss. The program gives that absence an interval: a section designated Latin in the score, with dispersed sound and a sparse visual trace. There is no reconstructed Latin transcription in the supplied texts. The three audible columns are Danish, German and English; the historical third language persists as a gap.

From system poem to inscription

I began working with system poetry in 2011. Sun: Triptychs for Solar Eclipses returns to that structure through a finite grid and its permitted combinations. Sand carries the relation between rule and utterance into a different material: an inscription whose words arrive already fixed, but whose letters can be sounded again under changing conditions.

Flora Excursoria Hafniensis brings Danish and Latin naming into contact in a historical botanical text. Sand extends that concern with languages inhabiting a shared surface. Its columns follow their own timings; their correspondences become events. Read beside Flora and Sun, the work can be understood as another situated encounter between a small formal system and a record that exceeds it.

DOS / return

The choice of MS-DOS also returns to an earlier part of my practice. NetLock BBS, which I ran in Hillerød in the 1990s, belonged to a culture of text screens, file descriptions and delayed exchanges. Archival work on the board prompted renewed experiments with that visual vocabulary in 2026.

Sand brings the monument into VGA graphics, Pascal, APL and an accompanying ANSI image. APL constructs the score and sound material; Pascal animates the reading and streams the supplied soundtrack. The code exposes the rule that links a character to a frequency. Publishing it lets that relation be inspected alongside the work's visible and audible forms.

The stone fixes the words. Their sounding takes time. What leaves the inscription remains in the sea.
